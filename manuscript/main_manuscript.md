## Abstract

**Objective.** To evaluate whether point and empirical interval forecasts of recorded exercise heart rate (HR) remain reliable across later sessions, unseen users, unseen sport families, and a separate public data source when information leakage is controlled.

**Approach.** A time-causal convolutional model used 5 min of HR, speed, and altitude signals plus optional summaries of completed workouts to forecast HR 1, 3, and 5 min ahead in a common complete-three-target cohort. Endomondo supported model fitting, selection, and conformalized quantile regression; GoldenCheetah provided frozen cross-source evaluation without adaptation or recalibration. Users and sessions were partitioned before windowing; metrics were averaged within session and user.

**Main results.** Relative to separately trained zero-history models, completed history reduced mean absolute error by 0.044--0.079 beats per minute (bpm) in 948 temporally evaluated users and by 0.029--0.112 bpm in 105 unseen users; the 1-min unseen-user 95% bootstrap interval (-0.056 to 0.006 bpm) included zero. Five-minute held-sport error ranged from 7.62 to 12.01 bpm. Under matched history-masked inference, median 90% interval coverage across five seeds was 0.892/0.887/0.877 internally and 0.880/0.859/0.850 across sources at 1/3/5 min, despite wider external intervals.

**Significance.** Forecast reliability depended more on sport and data-source boundaries than on architecture or completed-history personalization, showing that wearable-forecasting claims require explicit deployment and calibration boundaries. The findings concern observational device-recorded HR and do not establish clinical validity, device accuracy, or exercise-prescription benefit.

**Keywords:** heart-rate forecasting; wearable sensors; personalized modelling; distribution shift; conformal prediction; data leakage

## 1. Introduction

Wearable exercise records combine physiological, movement, and environmental signals at a scale rarely available in laboratory studies. They therefore support forecasts of the near-future heart rate (HR) that a device will record from information already observed during a workout. This task differs from contemporaneous or non-explicit-lead HR estimation, which reconstructs HR from another sensing modality [@reiss2019; @pacheco2024; @zhang2026]. A future forecaster must extrapolate individual HR dynamics while subsequent workload, terrain, stopping, and sensor artefact remain unknown.

Prior work addresses complementary parts of this problem. Exercise-HR forecasting studies include personalized mountain-biking models [@qiu2021], consumer-wearable interval-training trend forecasts [@fedorin2021], participant-independent short-lead forecasts from inertial and HR inputs [@zhu2022], and cycling forecasts that intentionally incorporate future course gradient [@gilbert2022]. Endomondo-based work modelled personalized activity and HR trajectories [@ni2019], while later models incorporated completed workouts or longer-term individual context [@kayange2024; @nazaret2023]. Other studies used univariate or autoregressive inputs [@namazi2022; @desabbata2025] or activity-conditioned multivariate context [@mateescu2025; @namazi2025]. Cross-study wearable analyses further show that monitoring models may not generalize between independently sourced cohorts [@kasl2024]. These strands establish short-lead prediction, personalization, participant-independent testing, activity conditioning, and predictive uncertainty separately. Evidence combining them under explicit temporal, unseen-user, held-sport, joint user--sport, and frozen cross-source boundaries remains limited (Supplementary Table S8); we make no claim of global priority.

Those boundaries matter because adjacent wearable windows share observations and repeated workouts retain user characteristics; random window splitting can therefore answer an easier question than deployment on a later session or a new person [@dehghani2019]. Leakage can also arise through train--test normalization, histories containing unfinished or future workouts, test-guided selection, or target-cohort recalibration [@kapoor2023]. Point accuracy alone does not show whether uncertainty remains reliable under such shifts. Quantile regression and conformalized quantile regression (CQR) provide predictive intervals [@koenker1978; @romano2019], but exchangeability cannot be assumed for arbitrary time-series or source shifts [@xu2023; @oliveira2024]. Calibration and accuracy should therefore be evaluated at the user level and on a source excluded from model selection, particularly because wearable HR varies with device, activity, motion, and participant characteristics [@shcherbina2017; @bent2020].

We therefore tested a general methodological proposition: a wearable forecast is reliable only relative to the information and population available at a declared deployment boundary. We conducted a leakage-controlled, multi-dataset study of past-only exercise HR forecasting (Fig. 1). Endomondo supplied model fitting, selection, normalization, and conformalized quantile regression (CQR) calibration; GoldenCheetah supplied a frozen cross-source evaluation after outcome-blind source-integrity, metadata-linkage, sport-harmonization, quality-eligibility, and duplicate checks [@endomondo2019; @goldencheetah2018]. First, we defined auditable information boundaries before window construction across temporal, user, sport-family, joint, and source shifts. Second, history-informed, history-masked, and zero-history-trained strategies separated the value of completed workouts from inference-time masking. Third, matched-information and sport-composition analyses tested whether point forecasts and Endomondo-derived empirical prediction intervals transported across sources. Finally, a retrospective negative control deliberately contaminated one strict-temporal pipeline with adjacent windows from test sessions while preserving the exact test origins. It measures only that contamination design and is not an estimate of general leakage bias.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Study design and data sources

This retrospective computational study used two publicly released, pseudonymized workout collections. Endomondo HR supplied all fitting, selection, normalization, and CQR calibration [@endomondo2019; @ni2019]. GoldenCheetah supplied the cross-source cohort and underwent outcome-blind source-integrity, linkage, sport, quality, and duplicate checks [@goldencheetah2018]. Its athlete-directory identifier defined the user. Within each directory, a CSV filename timestamp was linked to JSON ride metadata only when exactly one match arose after testing UTC offsets from -14:00 to +14:00 in 15-min steps; ambiguous, duplicate, unmatched, and invalid-metadata records were ineligible. This linked 50,002 of 51,470 CSVs (97.15%) across 150 directories; signal-quality and duplicate controls yielded 32,587 sessions from 144 users. Reference-seed GoldenCheetah results were inspected before the final five-seed reporting plan was consolidated; the cross-source evaluation was therefore not prospectively sequestered. Nevertheless, every reported checkpoint and its Endomondo calibration thresholds were frozen before the corresponding GoldenCheetah inference, and GoldenCheetah targets were not used for fitting, early stopping, adaptation, or recalibration. Observations were nested within sessions and users; recorded HR was the target rather than a clinical reference, and devices were heterogeneous. The workflow was protocol-led but not prospectively registered.

### 2.2. Session quality, duplicate control, and sport ontology

Sessions required a source user identifier, deterministic session key, 600--86,400 s duration, orderable timestamps, valid HR in at least 80% of samples, and a mappable sport label. HR outside 30--240 bpm was treated as missing; missing speed and altitude were represented by masks rather than future-derived filling.

Within each source, identifier-free fingerprints identified replicated sessions. Groups crossing users or conflicting sport families were excluded; a within-user, family-consistent group retained one deterministic canonical session. Cross-source overlap was screened using identifier- and absolute-time-invariant hashes of 10-s HR, speed, and altitude, followed by profile and locality-sensitive-hash candidates with continuous-signal verification. The audit tested declared transformations but could not exclude every conceivable transformed duplicate.

Outcome-blind label normalization mapped activities to outdoor cycling, indoor/virtual cycling, running, walking/hiking, swimming, skiing, strength/cross-training, or other/unknown. Five Endomondo families met the held-sport support threshold of 50 users and 1,000 sessions; the external analysis retained the three shared families: outdoor cycling, indoor/virtual cycling, and running. Ambiguous labels remained other/unknown and were not treated as a novel sport.

### 2.3. Past-only forecast origins and inputs

At origin \(t\), the context was the preceding 300 s in thirty right-closed 10-s bins. The last valid sample in each bin was retained; an empty bin remained zero-valued with its missingness mask set. The tensor contained HR, speed, and altitude plus observed/missing masks; derived speed used only earlier distance or position samples. Centered interpolation, backward filling, future distance, full-session duration, post-session summaries, and every other value after \(t\) were excluded.

Targets were recorded HR at \(t+60\), \(t+180\), and \(t+300\) s. An exact observation was preferred; otherwise, interpolation was allowed only between future HR samples no more than 30 s apart. Eligible origins required HR in at least 24 context bins, no boundary or internal HR gap longer than 60 s, and all three targets. Thus, every horizon used the same complete-three-target cohort, including the requirement that the 5-min target remained available. Candidates occurred every 60 s; primary reporting used 300-s spacing, with a frozen-checkpoint 60-s sensitivity analysis. Dense origins were repeated measurements, so metrics were aggregated first within session and then user. Full flow and support are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

### 2.4. Past-only completed-workout history

The history branch received 13 summaries of prior-session volume, duration, HR, speed, altitude variability, current-sport history, and recency; a mask distinguished absent history from numerical zeros (Supplementary methods; Table S9 reports availability). A workout became available only after ending no later than the current session start. For an unseen test user, an earlier completed workout in the chronological evaluation stream could update history for a later workout, but no gradient or network-parameter update occurred. Overlapping or future sessions were unavailable, held-sport sessions never updated history in leave-one-family-out experiments, and test-user history affected inference only.

### 2.5. Leakage-controlled evaluation protocols

Five protocols represented distinct deployment questions (Supplementary Table S1). **Strict temporal forecasting** divided ordered sessions within users into nonoverlapping train, validation, calibration, and test periods. **Unseen-user forecasting** assigned disjoint users before windowing; unseen means absent from parameter fitting, not necessarily without deployment-time history. **History-informed inference** supplied causally completed prior workouts to the history-capable model. **History-masked inference** used the same checkpoint but deliberately hid that input. A **zero-history-trained model** was independently trained and evaluated without prior-workout input. **Unseen-sport forecasting** excluded one supported family from training, validation, calibration, and history updates. Its input token was mapped to the learned other/unknown code 0; during held-family training, 10% sport-token dropout mapped nonheld tokens to the same code. **Joint shift** evaluated unseen users in that family; journal-facing outcome comparisons required at least 30 users per cell. **Frozen cross-source evaluation** applied Endomondo parameters and thresholds to the three shared GoldenCheetah sport families without adaptation or recalibration. GoldenCheetah inference was history-masked and therefore did not evaluate cross-source transport of the completed-history branch.

### 2.6. Baselines and main model

Past-only signal baselines were persistence, an exponentially weighted moving average selected on validation data, and a context linear trend. Learned comparators were XGBoost [@chen2016], a two-layer gated recurrent unit [@cho2014], a causal TCN [@bai2018], and a lightweight Transformer [@vaswani2017]. They shared the causal channels, masks, sport token, elapsed time, partitions, and residual target; neural models shared the epoch sampler and XGBoost used the corresponding hierarchical weights.

The main model used four residual causal TCN blocks to encode the current workout and a two-layer encoder for completed-history summaries. The current state, sport embedding, elapsed time, history representation, and presence mask entered a fusion head that estimated residual quantiles \(q\in\{0.05,0.10,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.90,0.95\}\) at each horizon. Residuals were added to the last context HR, restricted to 30--240 bpm, and sorted to prevent quantile crossing. Layer dimensions and resolved training settings are given in the Supplementary methods.

### 2.7. Training, model selection, and conformal calibration

Models were trained in PyTorch 2.11.0 with AdamW (initial learning rate 0.001; weight decay 0.0001) [@loshchilov2019]. Dynamic channels and history features were standardized from training data only, while hierarchical sampling and loss weighting balanced users and sessions. The main protocols sampled 500,000 origins per epoch; 20% history-mask dropout allowed the same fitted checkpoint to support history-informed and history-masked inference. Selection minimized their mean validation MAE. Each protocol used five configuration-declared random seeds (20260722--20260726), at most 40 epochs, and patience 4; every unseen-user checkpoint was frozen before seed-specific GoldenCheetah inference. As an independent probabilistic comparator, symmetric split-conformal intervals were centred on the deterministic persistence forecast using absolute residuals from the corresponding dedicated calibration partition. The 50%, 80%, and 90% radii used finite-sample higher order statistics; GoldenCheetah reused the unseen-user Endomondo radii unchanged.

To distinguish history supply from inference-time masking, one zero-history-trained model was fitted per protocol and seed, with prior-workout input absent throughout training, validation, and inference and selection by zero-history validation MAE. The ten models otherwise matched the main configuration and were frozen before cross-source inference. GRU and causal-TCN comparators used three matched seeds (20260722--20260724); XGBoost and Transformer were single-run secondary comparators. Held-sport models used three seeds. Test and cross-source outcomes never informed early stopping.

The quantile head was optimized by mean pinball loss. Central 50%, 80%, and 90% intervals used quantile pairs (0.25,0.75), (0.10,0.90), and (0.05,0.95), respectively. For each mode, level, and horizon, CQR calculated the maximum of lower-minus-observed and observed-minus-upper on the dedicated calibration partition. We used the standard higher empirical quantile over calibration origins and constrained the adjustment to be nonnegative, so calibration could expand but not narrow an interval [@romano2019]. Because this calculation pooled correlated origins rather than giving calibration users equal weight, it does not furnish a finite-sample guarantee for the session-then-user PICP estimand reported below; the interval results are therefore interpreted as empirical post-CQR calibration. GoldenCheetah used the Endomondo thresholds unchanged.

### 2.8. Outcomes and statistical analysis

The 5-min horizon was primary; 1- and 3-min horizons were secondary. Absolute and signed errors, interval scores, coverage indicators, widths, and pinball losses were averaged within session and then user; RMSE was calculated within session and subsequently averaged within user. MAE (bpm) was the primary point metric. Uncertainty metrics were PICP, absolute coverage error, interval width, pinball loss, WIS [@bracher2021], and the user-averaged within-user Spearman association between 90% width and absolute error. Optimization seeds were not treated as independent participants: results are reported per seed and as the median and range across the declared seed set.

The final analysis evaluated three five-seed strategy contrasts: history-informed minus history-masked inference from the history-capable model, history-masked inference minus the zero-history-trained model, and history-informed inference minus the zero-history-trained model. GoldenCheetah exposed only the latter two no-prior-workout strategies. Within each seed, paired losses were aggregated within session and then user; per-user differences were averaged across matched seeds before 10,000-replicate user resampling. The same design used three matched seeds for history-masked main-versus-GRU/TCN contrasts and history-informed held-sport main-versus-aligned-EWMA contrasts. A post hoc sport-availability sensitivity aligned history-masked full-sport and held-family predictions on identical joint-shift origins, then applied the same session--user--seed aggregation and user bootstrap; its held-minus-full contrast is operational rather than causal because token exposure, sport-specific fitting data, and locked training budgets also differed. Joint user--sport outcome comparisons were designated exploratory. To comply with the Physiological Measurement reporting threshold for measurements on human groups, the journal-facing outcome presentation includes only cells with at least 30 users; lower-support intersections remain in the immutable audit trail but contribute only support counts, not outcome estimates, to the submission figures and tables. These intervals quantify user-sampling variation conditional on the declared seed set; seeds were neither resampled nor used as inferential units. Reference-seed Holm-adjusted paired Wilcoxon tests [@wilcoxon1945; @holm1979] were retained as conditional sensitivity analyses rather than optimization-seed inference.

Robustness analyses covered HR-only input, 60-s origin spacing, and history masking. Calibration-estimand, source-composition, and external-sport sensitivities used frozen predictions. Equal-user/equal-session CQR thresholds were recomputed across all five unseen-user seeds; a separate reference-seed clustered analysis resampled calibration users and one session and origin per user. Point and interval metrics were compared under natural, matched-sport, and standardized sport compositions using independent user resampling within each source. These analyses were descriptive, not user-level guarantees or causal source effects.

For a deliberately invalid negative control, the original 104,144 strict-temporal test origins were fixed while 414,854 other 60-s origins from the same test sessions were assigned by a model-seed-independent SHA-256 rule: 290,245 entered fitting, 62,146 validation, and 62,463 calibration. Exact test rows remained disjoint, but overlapping context and target timestamps deliberately violated the session-before-window boundary. Three freshly initialized zero-history quantile TCNs used the formal training budget and were paired by seed and exact test-row order with the clean zero-history-trained strict-temporal models. Contaminated-minus-clean user effects were averaged over the three seeds before 10,000 user-bootstrap resamples. This negative control is invalid for generalization, has no conformal coverage guarantee, and is excluded from model rankings.

### 2.9. Sex and gender considerations

The source datasets provided a self-reported recorded-gender field rather than a verified measure of sex assigned at birth or gender identity. All eligible records were retained irrespective of this field, but the secondary datasets could not be balanced by sex or gender. The unseen-user test included only 10 users recorded as female, and GoldenCheetah metadata were more severely imbalanced; these strata did not meet the journal's 30-person group threshold. Consequently, no sex- or gender-group performance comparison is included in the journal-facing results. This imbalance limits population generalizability and prevents inference about sex- or gender-related forecast differences.

### 2.10. Reproducibility controls

Technical verification used versioned automated tests, independent metric recomputation, machine-linked number checks, and figure source-data exports. Quantitative figures were deterministically rendered from stored result tables; no participant measurements were synthesized or altered.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Cohort construction exposed substantial differences between datasets

Of 20,405,816 candidate origins, 7,635,176 (37.4%) met all context and target criteria. Acceptance was 29.1% in Endomondo and 81.8% in GoldenCheetah, reflecting sampling regularity and local target support. The unseen-user test contained 101,184 origins from 15,026 sessions and 105 users; the strict temporal test contained 104,144 origins from 16,012 sessions and 948 users. The three-family external analysis contained 531,725 origins from 31,851 sessions and 144 users. In the reference-seed source characterization, GoldenCheetah sessions were longer (114.8 versus 70.3 min), raw sampling gaps were shorter (median 1.04 versus 7.22 s), and mean targets were approximately 8.1--8.3 bpm lower than in the internal zero-history cohort; these are descriptive source differences, not causal effects (Supplementary Table S13). Users and sessions, rather than overlapping-origin counts, defined inferential support (Supplementary Table S1).

### 3.2. Completed history produced small gains over zero-history-trained models

Across five seeds, strict-temporal history-informed MAE medians were 6.012, 7.557, and 8.245 bpm at 1, 3, and 5 min, versus 6.067, 7.617, and 8.332 bpm for zero-history-trained models (Fig. 2a; Table 1). Seed-averaged user effects were -0.044 bpm (95% CI -0.059 to -0.029), -0.063 (-0.091 to -0.034), and -0.079 (-0.113 to -0.046). Within-checkpoint comparisons also favoured supplied history at every seed and horizon (Supplementary Table S6b).

In 105 unseen users, history-informed MAE medians were 5.849, 7.407, and 8.157 bpm, versus 5.866, 7.487, and 8.285 bpm for zero-history-trained models (Fig. 2b). Effects were -0.029 bpm (95% CI -0.056 to 0.006), -0.076 (-0.124 to -0.036), and -0.112 (-0.187 to -0.048): evidence was weak at 1 min but supported small 3- and 5-min gains.

Completed-workout history was available for every strict-temporal test session and for 14,925 of 15,026 unseen-user test sessions (99.3%; Supplementary Table S9). Median eligible prior-session counts were 269 (interquartile range 148--438) and 98 (41--196), respectively. Two of 105 unseen users never had an eligible prior workout and retained the explicit no-history state.

The main architecture did not uniformly dominate learned comparators. At 5 min, history-masked main-minus-GRU and main-minus-point-TCN effects were 0.063 bpm (95% CI 0.030 to 0.095) and 0.032 (0.006 to 0.058) in the strict temporal test, slightly favouring the comparators. In unseen users, the corresponding effects were 0.070 bpm (-0.002 to 0.151) and 0.033 (-0.012 to 0.091), both including zero. Full three-seed and secondary Transformer/XGBoost results are in Supplementary Tables S2--S3 and S6. The main model is supported by its completed-history and interval design, not broad point-forecast dominance.

### 3.3. Frozen cross-source evaluation showed lower natural-mix accuracy

Without GoldenCheetah adaptation, five-seed history-masked MAE medians were 7.465, 10.206, and 11.214 bpm (Fig. 2c). Seed-paired gaps relative to matched Endomondo unseen-user history-masked inference were 1.586 [1.517--1.601], 2.722 [2.684--2.737], and 2.911 [2.855--2.963] bpm. Zero-history-trained medians were 7.461, 10.218, and 11.226 bpm; history-masked-minus-zero-history-trained user effects were -0.006 bpm (95% CI -0.015 to 0.003), -0.014 (-0.029 to 0.002), and -0.024 (-0.041 to -0.008). The isolated 5-min difference was too small to support a broad training-strategy claim. At 5 min, history-masked main-minus-GRU and main-minus-point-TCN effects were -0.040 bpm (95% CI -0.070 to -0.010) and -0.030 (-0.050 to -0.011), again small. Deterministic persistence and EWMA remained worse overall. In the reference seed, equal-family standardization attenuated the cross-source MAE gaps to 0.500 bpm (95% CI -0.317 to 1.273), 1.438 (0.425 to 2.417), and 0.968 (-0.551 to 2.350), so natural-mix gaps reflected sport composition as well as other source differences (Supplementary Table S11). Architectures and training strategies showed similar natural-mix transport losses.

Cross-source performance varied by sport: 5-min history-masked median MAE ranged from 8.713 bpm for running to 11.564 bpm for outdoor cycling (Supplementary Table S3). Differences in users, devices, and exercise patterns preclude causal sport attribution.

### 3.4. Sport-family transfer was heterogeneous, and joint-shift support was uneven

Three-seed leave-one-family-out medians varied more across sports than the history ablation (Fig. 3). For seen users in an unseen sport, 5-min MAE ranged from 7.617 bpm for running to 12.012 bpm for strength/cross-training (Supplementary Table S5a).

Against aligned EWMA at 5 min, seed-averaged paired-user effects were -0.137 bpm (95% CI -0.250 to -0.023) for outdoor cycling, -0.413 (-0.698 to -0.139) for indoor/virtual cycling, 0.103 (0.045 to 0.162) for running, 0.965 (0.653 to 1.286) for walking/hiking, and 0.014 (-0.406 to 0.483) for strength/cross-training. Sport holdout therefore exposed activity-dependent baseline rankings concealed by aggregate unseen-user performance.

On identical joint-shift origins, the post hoc history-masked held-minus-full-sport 5-min MAE effects in cells meeting the 30-user reporting threshold were 0.350 bpm (95% CI 0.162 to 0.524; 77 users) for outdoor cycling and 0.590 (0.419 to 0.838; 88 users) for running (Supplementary Table S16). Indoor/virtual cycling, walking/hiking, and strength/cross-training contained only 18--20 users and were excluded from journal-facing outcome comparisons. The composite model differences preclude a universal or causal sport-withholding penalty.

Among the two joint user--sport cells meeting the reporting threshold, 5-min medians were 7.337 bpm for running and 10.879 bpm for outdoor cycling. These absolute errors were not consistently higher than the corresponding same-user held-sport medians, but the user sets were unmatched and this descriptive comparison does not estimate an additive joint-shift penalty. Exploratory model-minus-EWMA effects were -0.303 bpm (95% CI -0.726 to -0.008) for outdoor cycling and 0.334 (0.001 to 0.802) for running (Supplementary Tables S5a and S5c).

At 5 min, same-user held-sport median PICP ranged from 0.879 to 0.935. In the two reportable joint cells, PICP was 0.859 for outdoor cycling and 0.919 for running (Supplementary Table S5b). The three lower-support intersections are reported as support limitations, not outcome groups.

### 3.5. Empirical interval coverage was near nominal internally but lower across sources

Across five seeds, strict-temporal history-informed post-CQR 90% PICP medians were 0.896, 0.897, and 0.892 at 1, 3, and 5 min, with seed ranges no wider than 0.005 (Fig. 4a). Median widths increased from 24.77 to 33.64 bpm. For the information-matched comparison, unseen-user history-masked PICP medians were 0.892, 0.887, and 0.877, with widths of 23.57, 29.52, and 32.21 bpm (Fig. 4b). These are empirical prediction intervals: origin-pooled CQR does not provide a finite-sample guarantee for the session-then-user PICP estimand.

Frozen cross-source 90% PICP medians were 0.880, 0.859, and 0.850, while widths were 29.55, 37.50, and 40.56 bpm (Fig. 4c). Thus, at 5 min the nominal 90% interval covered about 85% of outcomes despite an average width of approximately 40.6 bpm. The corresponding seed ranges were 0.877--0.888, 0.853--0.863, and 0.846--0.854; these bars describe optimization-seed ranges, not confidence intervals. At 5 min, the independent persistence-conformal baseline had WIS values of 6.323, 6.387, and 8.323 in the strict-temporal, unseen-user, and cross-source regimes, compared with 4.912, 4.988, and 6.723 for the corresponding reference-seed main intervals (Supplementary Table S15). Its 90% PICP was 0.892, 0.879, and 0.811, respectively; external under-coverage therefore also occurred for the persistence-conformal baseline. In the reference seed, natural-mix cross-source WIS (lower is better) differences were 1.015, 1.681, and 1.771 bpm; equal-family standardization attenuated them to 0.479, 0.984, and 0.882 bpm, with the 1- and 5-min confidence intervals including zero (Supplementary Table S11). Across five seeds, equal-user/equal-session thresholds increased external 90% PICP to 0.892, 0.870, and 0.866 but widened intervals by approximately 0.86, 1.16, and 1.77 bpm on average; only 13 of 27 absolute-coverage-error sensitivity intervals excluded zero. Sport-specific and clustered-calibration results are reported in Supplementary Tables S10 and S12.

In the reference seed, mean within-user width--error Spearman correlations ranged from 0.263 to 0.361 across regimes and horizons (Fig. 4f). Width ranked some forecast difficulty but did not guarantee an individual prediction.

### 3.6. Ablations and sensitivity analyses bounded the main claims

Zero-history-trained analysis confirmed that inference-time masking within a history-capable checkpoint was not the main explanation for the completed-history effect (Supplementary Fig. 1c; Table S6b). Other sensitivities used reference seed 20260722. Multimodal-minus-HR-only intervals crossed zero internally; only the cross-source 5-min contrast excluded zero (-0.058 bpm, 95% CI -0.099 to -0.019), insufficient for a general multimodal-superiority claim.

The reference-seed dense-origin sensitivity contained 506,203 predictions, 5.00 times the 101,184 standard unseen-user origins. MAE changed by at most 0.111 bpm and 90% PICP by at most 0.004; no external sensitivity was performed.

A fixed-session target-availability sensitivity required only the evaluated horizon and added 2.7%–13.9% more origins across horizons and regimes. Across five frozen main-model seeds, median expanded-minus-common MAE changes ranged from -0.013 to 0.155 bpm; paired-user estimates ranged from -0.010 to 0.154 bpm. The 1-min internal paired-user confidence intervals included zero, whereas longer-horizon and cross-source expanded rows were modestly harder. All absolute median changes were at most 0.155 bpm, leaving the primary boundary ordering unchanged (Supplementary Table S18b).

In the deliberately contaminated strict-temporal negative control, 15,839 of 16,012 test sessions entered fitting; 85.7% and 98.9% of test origins had a contaminated fitting origin within 60 and 300 s, respectively; and 95.0% shared at least one target timestamp. Even under this invalid design, the three-seed median apparent MAE optimism was only 0.143%, 0.171%, and 0.070% at 1, 3, and 5 min. Paired user effects were -0.011 bpm (95% CI -0.025 to 0.002), -0.014 (-0.027 to -0.001), and -0.001 (-0.016 to 0.014), respectively. Across raw and post-CQR interval diagnostics, absolute PICP differences were at most 0.18 percentage points and mean-width differences at most 0.10 bpm (Supplementary Table S17). Thus, this specific contamination scheme produced no practically material or horizon-consistent point-accuracy benefit; it does not estimate the effects of other leakage mechanisms.

Recorded-gender metadata did not support the journal's minimum group size for a sex- or gender-stratified comparison; no such outcome comparison is presented.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Principal findings

This study provides three main findings. First, supplying completed prior-workout summaries yielded only 0.03--0.11-bpm user-level improvements relative to zero-history-trained models; the 95% user-bootstrap confidence interval for the 1-min unseen-user MAE difference included zero. Second, heterogeneity across sport families and the natural source mix was much larger. At 5 min, history gains were 0.079--0.112 bpm, the held-sport MAE range spanned 4.395 bpm, and the five-seed natural-mix cross-source gap was 2.911 bpm. In the reference seed, the corresponding natural-mix gap was 2.963 bpm and equal-family standardization yielded 0.968 bpm, with a confidence interval crossing zero. These descriptive magnitudes are not interchangeable causal effects, but they show which evaluation boundaries most changed the conclusion. Third, matched-information empirical intervals that were near nominal within Endomondo under-covered on GoldenCheetah despite being wider. The central result is therefore boundary-specific reliability, not a uniformly superior architecture.

### 4.2. Personalization should be defined by information available at deployment

Prior work has demonstrated that wearable HR trajectories contain user-specific structure [@ni2019; @hallgrimsson2018; @nazaret2023]. Our results support that premise but refine its operational meaning. An unseen user cannot have a learned training-time identity embedding, yet completed workouts may become available after enrolment. The history-informed protocol captured this setting without fine-tuning model parameters or exposing future sessions. Requiring a prior workout to have ended before the current workout began also eliminated a subtle route by which overlapping sessions could contaminate history.

History-informed versus zero-history-trained point estimates favoured completed history at all six internal regime--horizon combinations; intervals excluded zero for all temporal horizons and for unseen users at 3 and 5 min. Magnitudes remained small. The preceding 5-min HR trajectory already contains strong autoregressive information, and the 13 compact history summaries may not capture richer training state. For deployment, completed history may offer incremental bias correction rather than a dramatic accuracy gain. More complex adaptation should be tested under the same completed-session and unseen-user boundaries.

### 4.3. Frozen cross-source evaluation separated transportability from interval coverage

The frozen Endomondo model had lower GoldenCheetah MAE than the past-only signal baselines, although this comparison alone does not identify what transferred. Absolute error and interval width increased, and coverage fell below nominal at longer horizons under matched history-masked inference. Both the main model and GRU experienced nearly the same natural-mix penalty, so the shift was not solved by the quantile TCN architecture. Heterogeneous device measurement, athlete composition, sport mix, sampling, and platform-specific preprocessing are plausible contributors. Reference-seed equal-family standardization attenuated point-error and WIS gaps, while several confidence intervals crossed zero; composition was therefore important but not a complete explanation of all point estimates. Prior wearable and cross-study analyses show that measurement and model performance can vary with device, motion, participants, and cohort source [@shcherbina2017; @bent2020; @kasl2024], but the present data cannot identify which mechanism caused the shift. Accordingly, the aggregate difference is described as source- and composition-associated transport loss rather than an isolated platform or device effect. Because cross-source inference was history-masked, these results do not establish whether completed GoldenCheetah workouts would improve later forecasts.

Point and interval transportability diverged. Internally, the fitted quantile head and usually small or zero CQR expansions produced empirical coverage within approximately two percentage points of nominal. Externally, intervals widened substantially yet still under-covered by up to 5.8 percentage points at nominal 80% and 5.0 points at nominal 90%. This pattern is consistent with evidence that calibration may degrade under dataset shift [@ovadia2019]. Positive width--error correlations meant that wider intervals tended to coincide with larger errors, but the moderate associations do not make interval width a complete out-of-distribution detector. A practical system would need monitored, prospective recalibration on an appropriate target population, evaluated on users not used for that recalibration.

A non-clinical use case is a wearable interface displaying an expected short-term range for its recorded HR. An out-of-range value could prompt a sensor-quality or model-reliability check, not diagnosis or prescription. The study quantifies forecast and interval behaviour but does not validate such an interface or threshold.

### 4.4. Sport shift cannot be reduced to a single unseen-activity score

Running was easier than strength/cross-training in both same-user and joint-shift tests, and longer-horizon comparisons with EWMA changed sign for some families. These results likely combine physiological response, unobserved future workload, sensor placement, motion artefacts, session structure, and sampling differences. Because sport family was not randomized, the study cannot attribute error differences to sport physiology. The result is nevertheless operationally relevant: a pooled average would conceal activities in which the learned model failed to improve upon a simple past-only smoother.

The joint-shift analysis also illustrates why support should be reported rather than hidden behind an aggregate label. Three of five held-family intersections contained only 18--20 users. Their estimates are useful failure probes, especially the high strength/cross-training error, but they are too sparse for broad population claims. Future datasets should deliberately recruit users across multiple less-represented sports so that performance under user and sport boundaries can be estimated with adequate support.

### 4.5. The main contribution is evaluation control, not architectural novelty

The main model combined an established time-causal TCN, quantile regression, and conformal calibration [@bai2018; @koenker1978; @romano2019]. Across matched seeds, its point differences from GRU and point-TCN were small and varied by horizon and regime. The zero-history-trained strategy also changed MAE by only hundredths of a bpm. Presenting the network as uniformly superior would therefore misstate the evidence. Its value is the explicit information-availability design, ordered intervals, and evaluation across rigorously separated deployment boundaries.

The pipeline addressed copied signals, pre-split windowing, sessions crossing temporal partitions, active sessions or held sports entering history, validation statistics fit on test users, and target-source recalibration. The 60-s stride sensitivity reinforced that overlapping origins are repeated measurements, not independent evidence. The deliberately contaminated negative control directly measured one same-session-window failure mode, yet its paired MAE changes were no larger than 0.014 bpm. This near-null result is informative: leakage effects depend on the contamination mechanism, sampling weight, model, and deployment boundary. It neither validates window-level splitting nor quantifies exact-test reuse, test-fitted normalization, user leakage, or target-source adaptation. Leakage controls remain validity conditions for the reported contrasts. Similar controls may transfer to other biomedical time-series tasks nested within sessions and people.

### 4.6. Limitations

The study has important limitations. Both datasets contain self-selected platform users and observational workouts, so the evaluated populations are not representative samples of all exercisers. Device brands, sensor placement, firmware, and chest-strap versus optical measurement were incompletely documented. Recorded HR may include motion artefact and device error; the work forecasts the recorded signal rather than a synchronized electrocardiographic reference. GoldenCheetah is a separate external data source, but it is not a controlled device-validation cohort, and participant or device independence across sources could not be verified.

Future exercise intensity after the forecast origin was intentionally unavailable. This makes the task past-only but places an irreducible limit on 3- and 5-min accuracy, especially during intervals, stops, terrain changes, or resistance training. Requiring all three targets means that shorter-horizon results condition on availability of the other targets. The fixed-session sensitivity expanded support by 2.7%–13.9%; across five frozen main-model seeds, median MAE changes ranged from -0.013 to 0.155 bpm. This reduces but does not eliminate selection concern because sessions with no complete-three-target origin remained outside the diagnostic, and interval calibration was not reevaluated on the expanded rows. Sport families are broad operational categories, and residual label heterogeneity remains. Strength/cross-training and several joint-shift intersections had limited support. Swimming and skiing did not meet the protocol-defined analysis thresholds.

The history representation used aggregated completed-session features rather than raw longitudinal sequences. The unseen-user result mostly represents users absent from fitting but with substantial deployment-time history, whereas cross-source inference was history-masked; neither analysis characterizes early onboarding. Five seeds were used for the main and zero-history-trained strategies and three for GRU, point-TCN, and held-sport models; XGBoost, Transformer, and several sensitivities remained reference-seed analyses. Seed summaries describe the declared set, while user-bootstrap intervals condition on those seeds. The multimodal ablation showed little consistent speed/altitude benefit but does not establish physiological irrelevance. Recorded gender was incomplete, severely imbalanced externally, and not a complete sex-and-gender assessment.

Finally, standard split-conformal guarantees rely on exchangeability conditions that do not directly match clustered observational time series. Calibration records were separated from test records, and unseen-user calibration users were disjoint from test users, but the primary thresholds pooled correlated origins. Equal-user/equal-session thresholds improved median coverage at the cost of wider intervals without uniformly improving bootstrap uncertainty in absolute coverage error. Cross-source under-coverage is compatible with both calibration-estimand mismatch and broader source shift; the present analysis cannot isolate their contributions. Reference-seed GoldenCheetah results were inspected before the final five-seed reporting plan was consolidated, so this is a retrospectively frozen cross-source evaluation rather than a prospectively sequestered validation. Versioned configurations and per-checkpoint freezing improve traceability but do not replace preregistration. Prospective studies should evaluate horizon-specific eligibility, history-depth effects, user-clustered conformal methods, online recalibration, explicit device metadata, and predeclared target-population monitoring.

## 5. Conclusions

A past-only history-capable quantile forecaster showed small 1--5-min recorded-HR improvements relative to zero-history-trained models, with the weakest evidence at 1 min in unseen users. The dominant pattern was not architectural superiority but heterogeneous performance across sport families and the natural source mix. Under matched history-masked inference, Endomondo-derived empirical intervals under-covered on GoldenCheetah at longer horizons, while sport-composition standardization and alternative calibration weighting changed the magnitude but not the need for explicit boundary reporting. Wearable forecasting claims should therefore state user, session, sport, history, source, seed, and calibration boundaries. These findings apply to the observed platforms and do not establish clinical safety, physiological causality, device accuracy, or exercise-prescription benefit.
